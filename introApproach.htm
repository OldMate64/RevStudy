Over 1900+ years of Christian history, experience & scholarship — & cultural confusion, prejudices, conspiracy theories, money-making scams, a 16th century war & more recently a world-famous gun battle 😧 — a range of approaches to interpretation of biblical texts have developed, including for John's Apocalypse. A brief search on a web browser will soon identify 4 main approaches taken by commentators when interpreting Revelation.

The approach taken in this guide is built upon the following widely-accepted principles for sound exegesis of all scripture:
  • The primary meaning of any text is the meaning it held for the original author & audience.
  • That meaning is best understood by first considering the uses of the words, language & genre in the author's historical & cultural context.
  • This is particularly important for Revelation as it is framed not only as 'apocalypse' but as both a prophecy & a letter to 7 specific churches late in the 1st century Roman Empire.
  • Given the revelation is framed as what the Holy Spirit is saying to (all) the churches, we must also allow that the meaning & application of what is revealed may extend beyond the original context. Most theological traditions allow for a sensus plenior ('fuller sense') in biblical texts.
  • Any secondary &/or contemporary meanings for us, however, will be an application & extension (not a discontinuity or rejection) of the primary meanings the text would have had for the original audience, not a creative production of modern imagination &/or prejudice which has little or no connection with what was revealed to the original audience.

Hence, the overall approach taken in this guide is a 'literary' reading of the Apocalypse. This may be understood as 'grammatico-historical exegesis' without limiting that to a 'literalist' reading of the text.

'Literalist' readings typically fail to fully take into account the original cultural complexity or the range of strategies used by the extremely creative writer of this often-breath-taking & carefully constructed text. Such approaches are also often a denial of the Holy Spirit's intent to communicate revelation to those first (& subsequent) readers, not just to idiocentric 21st century Christians on the other side of the planet! Literalist readings also tend to impose upon the text meanings which clearly come not from the context of the original author or audience, but from the context of modern readers; this leads to 'eisogesis' rather than 'exegesis'.
